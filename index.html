This archive begins with the people named by the family and the published history of a Salvadoran aviator whose life intersected with the April 1944 uprising.

Family materials identify José Gavidia and Carlota Castro de Gavidia as the parents of a ten-child family associated with Santa Ana; the 1944 prison registry names Carlos’s parents as José Gavidia and Carlota Castro. Whether Carlota was the same person as Carlota Antonia Castro Bernal remains unconfirmed. Why this matters — April 1944

This was not an isolated family tragedy. Carlos and José Antonio were caught in one of the defining confrontations of twentieth-century Salvadoran history.

On Palm Sunday, April 2, 1944, military officers rose against General Maximiliano Hernández Martínez after thirteen years of dictatorship. The failed uprising and the executions that followed helped ignite the nonviolent general strike that forced the dictator from power five weeks later. Documented

How big was it?

The uprising is known as the Palm Sunday Coup or rebelión del 2 de abril de 1944. It failed within days; its leaders were hunted down and tried by Consejos de Guerra. Scholarship based on the certified court-martial file counts 44 death sentences, with 14 carried out—13 military men and one civilian. Scholarship

The reprisals outraged the country. In May, the massive Huelga de Brazos Caídos forced Hernández Martínez to resign on May 9. The event remains one of the best-known episodes in Salvadoran history, and the novel’s epilogue recounts that May strike. DocumentedNovel

The reprisals: a corroborating roster

A later eyewitness recollection by someone who was eight years old in Villa El Rosario in 1944 independently names both brothers—“capitán Carlos Gavidia Castro” and “teniente Antonio Gavidia Castro”—among those shot. It also names Capitán Carlos Francisco Piche; Tenientes Alfonso Marín, Marcelino Calvo, Miguel Ángel Linares, Ricardo Mancía, Oscar Armando Cristales and Edgardo Chacón; Capitán Manuel Sánchez Dueñas; Coronel Tito Tomás Calvo; Mayor Julio Faustino Sosa; and General Alfonso Marroquín. The key names align with the court-martial scholarship. Later eyewitness account

The account also names civilian Víctor Marín, described as “un santo laico,” as shot one April morning; Tenientes Mario Villacorta and Héctor Cárdenas as killed by military patrols; and journalist Jorge Pinto as machine-gunned in his cell. Later eyewitness account

How the strike brought the dictatorship down

AGEUS began the university strike on April 28; factories and rail service stopped on May 2; banks and shops closed May 3; and public employees joined May 4. On May 7, security agent Juan Arnoldo Reyes Baires killed José Wright Alcaine, prompting a protest from U.S. Ambassador Walter Thurston. On May 9, Martínez handed power to Vice President Andrés Ignacio Menéndez and fled to Guatemala. Later eyewitness account

On May 15, 1966, Hernández Martínez was assassinated at his Jamastrán farm in Honduras by his own aide, Cipriano Morales. Later eyewitness account

Early life and aviation. Born the second of ten children, Carlos entered the Escuela Militar on February 13, 1931, at age 15; became subteniente on October 20, 1933, with the IV Promoción; entered the Aviation School at Ilopango in 1936; and became teniente on September 29. He earned his pilot title January 5, 1937, and flew the WACO CTO, Curtiss-Wright CW-14 “Osprey” and Caproni AP-1. Biography

Family and career. Carlos and María Teresa married civilly around 1937. Their first three children were born during this marriage, and they were expecting a fourth in April 1944. Suspicion surrounding the family led to his forced “voluntary” discharge from aviation in late 1939; he managed Haciendas El Ángel and El Carmen, rejoined aviation in late 1942 flying the Fairchild PT-19 and Vultee BT-13A, and became captain on November 12, 1943. Biography

What he did in April 1944. Persuaded by José Antonio, Carlos joined the uprising. Loyalist roadblocks kept him from Ilopango, so he joined Subteniente Juan Ramón Merino’s armored ground unit and fought around Candelaria and Cuartel San Carlos; the biography finds no evidence that he flew a bombing mission. Retreating toward Santa Ana, his vehicle ran out of fuel near San Andrés. He escaped through cane fields, reached Hacienda El Ángel for clothes and money, and tried to reach Honduras, but his paid guide betrayed him to a National Guard patrol near the border. He was returned to the Central Penitentiary. Biography

Marriage, sentence and execution. On the morning he was to be executed, Carlos asked to marry María Teresa in the Catholic Church because he did not want to die without that sacrament. With special archdiocesan permission, they contracted an in articulo mortis marriage inside the prison. The registry’s 5:15 a.m. notation is the ceremony time, not an execution time. Registry The biography reports an April 22 sentence; the court-martial study lists him among those condemned on April 23. He was captured and condemned to death, then survived to write this first-hand account of the revolt, its defeat, the prisons and courts-martial, and the repression that followed. The coreEl núcleo

Capitán Carlos Gavidia Castro, a military pilot, joined the April 2, 1944 uprising against dictator Maximiliano Hernández Martínez. It failed. He was court-martialed and executed, along with his brothers José Antonio—“Rey Antonio”—and Juan Carlos. The morning Carlos died, he and María Teresa González married in articulo mortis inside the Central Penitentiary.El capitán Carlos Gavidia Castro, piloto militar, participó en el levantamiento del 2 de abril de 1944 contra el dictador Maximiliano Hernández Martínez. El intento fracasó. Fue sometido a consejo de guerra y ejecutado, junto con sus hermanos José Antonio—«Rey Antonio»—y Juan Carlos. La mañana en que Carlos murió, él y María Teresa González se casaron in articulo mortis dentro de la Penitenciaría Central.

The bookEl libro

María Teresa told the story in Pugna a un dictador (1987). She said plainly that she changed the names of living protagonists, while printing the real execution lists in the front matter and sometimes letting real names surface in quoted speech. The code is now partly cracked: Romo = Gavidia, Gutiérrez = Calvo, and about 25 identities have been mapped; see the pseudonym key below.María Teresa contó la historia en Pugna a un dictador (1987). Explicó claramente que cambió los nombres de los protagonistas vivos, mientras imprimía las listas reales de ejecutados en las páginas preliminares y a veces dejaba aparecer nombres verdaderos en el diálogo citado. El código ya está parcialmente descifrado: Romo = Gavidia, Gutiérrez = Calvo y se han trazado unas 25 identidades; véase la clave de seudónimos más abajo.

The networkLa red

This was not a lone rebellion. Military families were intertwined through classmates such as Flores Magaña, compadres such as Capitán Erazo—who refused to lead the firing squad—and in-laws such as Erazo’s brother-in-law Marín, who was also executed. Marta Pérez, the bride Carlos’s family wanted him to marry, was widowed that same April when her husband Flores Magaña was executed.No fue una rebelión aislada. Las familias militares estaban entrelazadas por compañeros como Flores Magaña, compadres como el capitán Erazo—quien se negó a dirigir el pelotón de fusilamiento—y parientes políticos como Marín, cuñado de Erazo, también ejecutado. Marta Pérez, la novia con quien la familia quería casar a Carlos, quedó viuda ese mismo abril cuando ejecutaron a su esposo, Flores Magaña.

The survivorsLos sobrevivientes

Dagoberto served as consul in San Francisco, resigned in protest and returned after the dictator fell. Estela became the first woman to earn a doctorate from UES; Ricardo became a dean there; Juan José lived to 104. The survivors rebuilt through education and public service.Dagoberto fue cónsul en San Francisco, renunció en protesta y regresó después de la caída del dictador. Estela se convirtió en la primera mujer en obtener un doctorado en la UES; Ricardo llegó a ser decano allí; Juan José vivió hasta los 104 años. Los sobrevivientes reconstruyeron sus vidas por medio de la educación y el servicio público.

The Guirola family storyLa historia de la familia Guirola

The Guirolas were coffee-era royalty—among the richest families in El Salvador and owners of large parts of Santa Tecla. Rafael María Guirola was a younger son from Santander, Spain, cut out of the inheritance by custom. He emigrated and rebuilt from nothing, then married Gertrudis de la Cotera y González—also from a Santander family—in Zacatecoluca in 1808.Los Guirola eran la realeza de la época cafetalera: una de las familias más ricas de El Salvador y propietaria de grandes extensiones de Santa Tecla. Rafael María Guirola era un hijo menor de Santander, España, excluido de la herencia por costumbre. Emigró y reconstruyó su fortuna desde cero; en 1808 se casó en Zacatecoluca con Gertrudis de la Cotera y González, también de una familia santanderina.

Their son Ángel Guirola (1826–1910) served as acting president of El Salvador in 1884 and founded the Banco Internacional. He married Cordelia Duke Alexander of Kingston, Jamaica, and all their children carried the compound surname Guirola Duke.Su hijo Ángel Guirola (1826–1910) ejerció como presidente interino de El Salvador en 1884 y fundó el Banco Internacional. Se casó con Cordelia Duke Alexander, de Kingston, Jamaica, y todos sus hijos llevaron el apellido compuesto Guirola Duke.

One of those sons, Ángel Guirola Duke, ran for president in 1919. On the night of January 22, 1932, he co-founded the council that organized the repression of the peasant uprising—the Matanza—and became a leader in Hernández Martínez’s own party.Uno de esos hijos, Ángel Guirola Duke, se postuló a la presidencia en 1919. La noche del 22 de enero de 1932 cofundó el consejo que organizó la represión del levantamiento campesino—la Matanza—y llegó a ser dirigente del propio partido de Hernández Martínez.

The historical irony: if the cousin’s claim is true, the captain’s biological grandfather was a pillar of the Martínez regime—the same regime that executed the captain in 1944 for rebelling against it.La ironía histórica: si la afirmación del primo es cierta, el abuelo biológico del capitán fue un pilar del régimen de Martínez, el mismo régimen que ejecutó al capitán en 1944 por rebelarse contra él.

Research Action Plan

A practical path for separating family memory from documented fact and finding the records that can close the gaps.

Red flags

  • Unproven: that the family owned Haciendas El Ángel and El Carmen. The current record says Carlos administered them; management is not proof of ownership.
  • Unproven: that El Ángel and El Carmen became the Talcualuya cooperative.
  • Disputed: the biography gives April 24 for Carlos’s execution, while some popular summaries report April 26 and the novel includes conflicting dates.
  • Still checking: the exact execution time. The registry’s 5:15 a.m. notation is the marriage ceremony time; the family biography gives a 6:30 a.m. execution at the Cementerio General.

What you can do

  • Compare the novel’s two war-council rosters with the certified 1944 trial file.
  • Ask older relatives to resolve the portrait’s 1936/1937 date and “our grandfather in the middle” identification, then confirm all ten children.
  • Ask for Capitán Erazo’s first name and the date, place and circumstances of Juan Carlos’s death in 1944.

Order to request documents

  1. CNR: Datos de Índice and Certificación Literal for the hacienda lands.
  2. ISTA: expropriation decrees for the Talcualuya area.
  3. Diario Oficial, 1944: any confiscation decrees issued after the April uprising.
  4. Municipal catastros: San Juan Opico and San Pablo Tacachico.
  5. Santa Ana parish registers: search through FamilySearch.

Items 1–4 require in-person visits in El Salvador.
